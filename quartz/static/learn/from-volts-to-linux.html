1

Bits and signals

voltage → 1/0 → numbers, letters, colors, sound

Everything a computer stores or does is made of bits. A bit is a single yes/no value, written 1 or 0. Physically it is a voltage on a tiny wire: clearly high means 1, clearly low means 0. That's all a computer has to work with.

One bit can only say two things. But bits multiply: 2 bits give 4 patterns (00, 01, 10, 11), 3 bits give 8, and every extra bit doubles the count. 8 bits, called a byte, give 256 patterns. That is enough for every letter, digit and symbol on your keyboard, or for 256 shades of one color.

Binary counting works like normal counting, but each column is worth double the one to its right instead of ten times. In decimal, 305 means 3×100 + 0×10 + 5×1. In binary, 101 means 1×4 + 0×2 + 1×1 = 5. Try it on the bench: click the bits and watch the same pattern turn into a number, a letter and a shade of grey.

Why only two values?

Here's the part we talked about: underneath, the world is analog. The voltage on a wire is a smooth, continuous quantity, and it wobbles with heat and electrical noise. So chips use thresholds. Above a "high" line counts as 1, below a "low" line counts as 0, and the zone in between is forbidden. As long as noise doesn't push a signal across that gap, the bit reads perfectly.

That's the real reason computers are digital. Ten possible voltage levels would pack more information per wire, but they'd sit so close together that the smallest noise would turn a 6 into a 7. With two levels far apart, the chip can ignore almost all noise, and a copy of a copy of a copy is still perfect.

Turning the analog world into bits: sound

Sound is air pressure going up and down smoothly. A microphone turns it into a smooth voltage. To store it as bits, the computer does two things:

  1. Sampling: measure the wave at regular moments. CD audio measures 44,100 times per second.
  2. Quantization: round each measurement to the nearest allowed level. CD audio uses 16 bits per measurement, so 65,536 levels.

The surprising part is the Nyquist–Shannon theorem: if you sample more than twice as fast as the fastest wiggle in the sound, the samples hold enough information to rebuild the wave exactly, not approximately. Human hearing tops out around 20,000 wiggles per second (20 kHz), which is why 44,100 was chosen. Only the rounding step loses anything, and at 16 bits that error is about 96 dB quieter than the loudest sound.

Below Nyquist, something strange happens: the samples can't tell a fast wiggle from a slow one, and the rebuilt sound contains a wrong, lower tone. That is called aliasing. It's the same effect that makes car wheels look like they spin backwards in videos.

Transistors and logic gates

switch → gate → adder → ALU

Bits are voltages. To do something with them, you need switches that can be controlled by other bits. That switch is the transistor, and it's the single most important invention behind computers.

A transistor has three connections. Current wants to flow from the source to the drain, but a third connection, the gate, decides whether it can. Put voltage on the gate and the path opens. Take it away and the path closes. No moving parts: the gate's voltage changes how the silicon itself conducts.

A water pipe with a valve, where the valve is opened by water pressure from a different pipe. Because the "valve" is controlled by the same thing that flows through the pipe, the output of one switch can open the next one. That's what lets you chain billions of them.

What the silicon is made of. Pure silicon barely conducts. Chipmakers mix in a tiny amount of other atoms, which is called doping. Phosphorus adds spare electrons that can move freely: that's n-type silicon (n for negative). Boron leaves gaps where an electron is missing, called holes, and they move around like positive charges: that's p-type. A transistor is a sandwich of the two, n then p then n, with the gate sitting on top of the p part. Its mirror image (p, n, p) switches on when the gate gets 0 instead of 1, and chips pair the two kinds. That pairing is why chips are called CMOS.

A transistor, close up

This is an n-channel transistor (NMOS), the kind chips are full of. Simple shows what it does: a switch. Inside the silicon cuts it open from the side to show how it does it. Click the gate to put voltage on it, then flip between the two views.

Modern transistors are made in silicon and are almost unimaginably small. Your i7-10750H was made on Intel's 14 nm process and has several billion of them; a recent phone chip has over 15 billion. They switch billions of times per second.

Gates: switches wired into logic

Wire two transistors in a row (series) and current only gets through if both are on. That's an AND gate. Wire them side by side (parallel) and current gets through if either is on. That's an OR gate. A slightly different arrangement flips the input: NOT. These tiny circuits are called logic gates, and they're where electricity starts behaving like logic.

The gate doesn't "understand" AND. The wires are simply arranged so that electricity physically can't get through unless both switches are closed.

From gates to arithmetic

Here is the "aha" moment of computer science. Adding two single bits has only four cases. In binary, 1 + 1 = 10 (two), so you write 0 and carry 1, just like 5 + 5 = 10 in decimal.

ABSumCarry
0000
0110
1010
1101

Look at the columns. Sum is 1 only when the inputs differ: that's exactly XOR. Carry is 1 only when both are 1: that's exactly AND. So one XOR plus one AND is an adding machine for one column. It's called a half adder.

Half adder

bench 2.3

Flip A and B. The same two inputs feed both gates.

To add bigger numbers, chain them. A full adder adds two bits plus the carry coming from the column to its right. Line up four and you can add 4-bit numbers; line up 64 and you have the adder inside your CPU. The carry ripples from right to left exactly like when you add on paper.

4-bit adder, like on paper

bench 2.4

Click bits in the two top rows. Carries (dashed) ripple leftward. 4 bits can hold 0 to 15; go above 15 and the fifth column lights up, which is called an overflow.

Everything else is built from this

  • Subtraction is adding a negative number, stored with a trick called two's complement, so the same adder is reused.
  • Multiplication is shifting and adding, like long multiplication.
  • Comparison ("is A bigger than B?") is subtracting and checking whether the result is negative or zero.
  • Choosing is done by gates that route a signal one way or another (a multiplexer).

Bundle all of these into one block and you get the ALU (Arithmetic Logic Unit), the part of the CPU that actually calculates. One more surprising fact: the NAND gate (AND followed by NOT) is universal. You can build every other gate, and therefore a whole computer, out of NANDs alone. The free course Nand to Tetris does exactly that, step by step.

3

Memory

loops that remember · the clock · registers · RAM · flash

Every circuit in Layer 2 only reacts. An AND gate's output depends on its inputs right now. Change the inputs and the old answer is gone. A machine made only of these could add, but it couldn't remember the result, keep a program, or know which step it's on.

The trick that gives circuits memory is a loop: feed a circuit's output back into its own input. Two gates wired in a circle can hold each other in place, like two people leaning back to back. Neither one needs pushing; the pair stays put. That stable state is a stored bit.

Give that loop two control wires, Set and Reset, and you can change what it holds. This circuit is called an SR latch. A normal gate is like a doorbell button: it works only while pressed. A latch is like a light switch: it stays where you left it.

SR latch: a bit that remembers

bench 3.1

Two NOR gates (OR followed by NOT), each feeding the other. Press Set: Q becomes 1 and stays 1 after you let go. Press Reset: Q becomes 0 and stays. Then cut the power.

The clock: everyone saves on the beat

A CPU has billions of these storage cells, and signals race through gates at slightly different speeds. If every cell saved whenever its input changed, some would catch half-finished results. The fix is a clock: one signal that ticks between 0 and 1 at a steady rate, sent to every storage cell.

A flip-flop is a latch that only copies its input at the exact moment the clock rises. Between ticks, the gates calculate and the results settle. On the tick, every flip-flop saves at once. Your i7's clock runs up to 5.0 GHz in boost, which is 5 billion ticks per second.

An orchestra and a conductor. Musicians can prepare their next note whenever they like, but they all play it on the beat.

Flip-flop timing diagram

bench 3.2

Top: the clock. Middle: the data input D, which you control. Bottom: the stored output Q. Flip D at random moments and notice that Q only changes on a rising clock edge (dashed lines), never in between.

Put 64 flip-flops side by side on the same clock and they hold one 64-bit number. That's a register. Registers live inside the CPU and are its fastest memory; an x86-64 core has 16 general-purpose ones. They're the CPU's hands: numbers come into registers, go through the ALU, and the result lands in another register, all in one tick.

Different memories, different physics

A flip-flop needs about 6 to 20 transistors per bit, which is too expensive for gigabytes. So computers use several kinds of memory, each storing a bit in a different physical way:

Leaky buckets: why RAM forgets

bench 3.3

Eight DRAM cells storing one byte. Click a cell to write 1 or 0. Charge leaks away constantly; a reader sees 1 only above the halfway line. Turn off refresh and watch the byte decay. Time is slowed down: one second here is about 64 ms in a real chip.

This answers the puzzle from the boot explanation: at power-on RAM is empty because its buckets leaked away, while the firmware survives because its electrons are physically trapped in the flash chip. So the CPU must start from the flash chip and load everything else into RAM.

The memory hierarchy

Fast memory is small and expensive; big memory is slow. So computers stack them: the CPU keeps what it needs right now in registers, recently used data in cache, the running programs in RAM, and everything else on the SSD. The gap in speed is enormous, and it's hard to feel in nanoseconds, so the bench can stretch time to human scale.

Addresses

RAM has billions of cells. Each location has a number, its address, like house numbers on a street. To read, the CPU puts the address on a set of wires; a circuit of gates called a decoder opens only the cells at that address, and their bits travel back on other wires. "Load from address 200" is physically exactly that.

The CPU

stored program · fetch → decode → execute · jumps

We have something that calculates (the ALU) and something that remembers (registers and RAM). The last ingredient is control: a way for the machine to decide, by itself, what to do next. That's what turns parts into a computer.

The key idea, from the 1940s, is the stored program (often called the von Neumann architecture): put the instructions in the same memory as the data. A program is a list of numbers sitting in RAM. To run a different program, load different numbers. That's why the same laptop can be a game console one minute and a CAD workstation the next.

What an instruction is

Each instruction is a bit pattern meaning one tiny action: copy a number from memory into a register, add two registers, store a register to memory, jump somewhere else. The first part, the opcode, says which action; the rest says which registers or addresses to use. The full list a CPU understands is its instruction set. Your Intel CPU speaks x86-64; phones and recent Macs speak ARM. The same bits mean different things to each, which is why programs are built for one or the other.

Two special parts

  • The Program Counter (PC) is a register holding the address of the next instruction. It's the CPU's bookmark.
  • The Control Unit is a block of gates that reads the opcode and flips the right internal switches: which registers connect to the ALU, whether the ALU adds or subtracts, where the result goes.

Then the CPU repeats one cycle, forever, as long as there's power: fetch the instruction at the PC's address, decode it, execute it, and advance the PC to the next one. Step through it below.

Jumps make a computer more than a calculator

If the PC only ever went up by one, a computer could only follow a straight list. The jump instruction overwrites the PC with a new address. An unconditional jump makes a loop. A conditional jump ("if this register is zero, jump to 107") makes a decision. With load, store, arithmetic and conditional jumps, a computer can compute anything that is computable at all. Alan Turing proved this in the 1930s, before real computers existed. Every app you've used, including a chatbot, reduces to these few kinds of steps repeated billions of times.

How real CPUs go faster

Our simulator does one phase at a time. Real CPUs overlap them like an assembly line: while one instruction executes, the next is being decoded and the one after that fetched. This is pipelining.

Firmware and boot

power → UEFI → GRUB → kernel → systemd → you

Now we can explain booting physically. When power arrives, RAM is empty (its buckets leaked), and the CPU starts its fetch-decode-execute cycle immediately. It needs an instruction from somewhere. CPUs are built so that at power-on the PC points to one fixed address, and the motherboard wires that address to a flash chip. The program stored there is the firmware.

Firmware is software that lives permanently inside hardware, hence the name: firmer than software. On PCs it's called UEFI (older machines had a BIOS). It tests the hardware, sets it up, and then looks for a boot program on a disk. Every step after that follows the same pattern: load the next, more capable program into RAM, then jump to it. That chain is why it's called booting, from "pulling yourself up by your bootstraps".

Where everything lives on the disk

UEFI understands disk partitions. It looks for a small partition formatted as FAT32 called the EFI System Partition (ESP), which holds boot programs as .efi files. Windows has its boot manager there. On your MSI, the CachyOS installer wouldn't use the 200 MB Windows ESP (it wants at least 512 MB), so CachyOS got its own 1 GB ESP, and GRUB's file grubx64.efi lives there. A disk can hold more than one ESP: the firmware's boot list points at each one. GRUB keeps the kernel itself on the Linux partition in /boot, so its ESP only needs a small file; systemd-boot, the alternative, keeps the kernels on the ESP.

GRUB, the receptionist

GRUB shows a menu: CachyOS, an older kernel, a snapshot, or Windows. If you don't choose, it boots the default after a few seconds. When you pick an entry, it loads two files into RAM: the kernel (vmlinuz) and the initramfs, a tiny temporary filesystem with the drivers the kernel needs to reach your real disk (NVMe, Btrfs, encryption). Then it jumps into the kernel and its job is over.

You can press e on an entry to see and temporarily edit the exact commands GRUB will run. That's how you add a kernel option or boot into recovery when something breaks. Try it on the mock-up below.

The operating system

privilege · system calls · interrupts · scheduling · virtual memory

The kernel is running. Now the hard problem: one CPU (well, 12 threads), one RAM, one SSD, and dozens of programs that all want them at the same time. Without rules, every program would need its own driver for every device, and any bug would overwrite another program's memory. The operating system's kernel solves this by being the only program allowed to touch the hardware, and by sharing it fairly between everyone else.

Privilege is enforced by the CPU itself

The CPU has a mode switch built into its circuits. In kernel mode (called ring 0 on x86) every instruction is allowed. In user mode (ring 3), instructions that touch hardware or change memory mappings are forbidden: if a program tries one, the CPU stops it and hands control to the kernel. So even a buggy or malicious app physically can't write straight to your SSD. This is the "head chef" role, enforced in silicon.

System calls: the only door into the kernel

So how does a program read a file if it can't touch the disk? It asks. It puts a request number and its arguments into registers and runs a special instruction, syscall. The CPU switches to kernel mode and jumps to one fixed entry point the kernel set up at boot. The kernel checks the request, does the work, and returns to user mode. Programs can ask; they can never barge in.

Interrupts: how hardware gets attention

Instead of the CPU asking the keyboard "pressed yet?" millions of times, the keyboard sends an interrupt: an electrical signal that makes the CPU pause, save where it was, and run a kernel handler. Disks, network cards and a timer do the same. Follow a whole file read through the layers:

    Multitasking: switching faster than you can notice

    Your laptop runs hundreds of processes on 12 hardware threads. The trick is the timer interrupt: many times per second it gives control back to the kernel, whose scheduler decides who runs next. Switching means saving one process's registers and PC and loading another's, a context switch. It happens thousands of times per second, so everything appears to run at once.

    Most processes aren't even asking for the CPU most of the time. A process is running (on a core now), ready (wants a core, waiting its turn) or waiting (blocked on something: a key, a disk read, a network packet). When the thing it waits for arrives, an interrupt marks it ready again. Linux's scheduler since kernel 6.6 is called EEVDF; CachyOS ships tuned variants that favor responsiveness for desktop and gaming.

    Virtual memory: every process thinks it's alone

    Each process sees its own private address space, starting near 0. These are virtual addresses. A piece of CPU hardware called the MMU translates them, in chunks called pages (usually 4 KB), into real RAM locations the kernel chose. Two programs can both use "address 0x2000" and never collide, because each one's 0x2000 maps to a different physical place.

    • One process can't see or break another's memory, so one crashing app doesn't take down the rest.
    • Shared code, like the C library, can be mapped into many processes but stored in RAM only once.
    • Rarely used pages can be moved to swap on disk; touching one causes a page fault, and the kernel quietly brings it back.
    • Touching an address that isn't mapped at all is a segmentation fault: the kernel stops the process.

    "Everything is a file"

    Linux shows devices and kernel information as files. Your SSD is /dev/nvme0n1. Every process has a folder in /proc (systemd's is /proc/1/). Hardware details live in /sys. That's why so many Linux tools are just ways of reading files, and why cat /proc/cpuinfo works.

    On your machines

    • First install two tools CachyOS doesn't include: sudo pacman -S htop strace
    • htop: every core, every process, memory and swap. Press F5 for a tree that starts at PID 1, and q to quit.
    • vmstat 1 5: one line per second, five times. Skip the first line (it's an average since boot). in is interrupts per second, cs is context switches per second, id is how idle the CPU is (%).
    • strace -c ls: every system call ls makes, counted.
    • head /proc/self/maps: a real virtual memory map, of the head command itself (/proc/self always means "the process reading this"). Each line is one region: its address range, permissions (r read, w write, x execute), and the file it comes from. Run it twice: the addresses change every time (that's ASLR, a security feature).
    • Your Proxmox LXC containers are this same idea pushed further: processes on one shared kernel, each given its own isolated view.

    1. What stops a normal app from writing straight to the SSD?
      Show answerThe CPU runs it in user mode, where hardware instructions are forbidden. Only the kernel, in kernel mode, can touch devices.
    2. Why does the kernel need a timer interrupt?
      Show answerTo take the CPU back from programs that would never give it up, so the scheduler can share it fairly.
    3. On bench 6.3, why does the terminal use no CPU until you press a key?
      Show answerIt's waiting on keyboard input. Only the keyboard interrupt makes it ready again.
    4. On bench 6.4, both processes use libc but it's in RAM once. How?
      Show answerBoth page tables map their libc pages to the same physical frame. Each process still sees it at its own virtual address.
